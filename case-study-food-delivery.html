Food Delivery: The Moderate User Problem

A product teardown of Zomato's engagement mechanics and a proposed strategy to improve order frequency among mid-tier users — the highest-ROI growth segment in the platform.

Type Product Teardown — Independent Analysis
Subject Zomato (India)
Domain Consumer Tech / Growth / Engagement
Period 2024
Independent product analysis. Not affiliated with or commissioned by Zomato. All data sourced from publicly available reports and product audits.

01
Problem Product Teardown — Independent Analysis

Zomato's growth problem is not acquisition — it's depth

Zomato has approximately 17 million monthly transacting users (publicly reported). Heavy users (9+/month) are saturated; new user CAC is prohibitively high. The highest ROI opportunity lies in converting moderate users (4–8/month) into heavy users — a frequency play, not an acquisition play.

User Segment Distribution — Relative Scale

Light Users (1–3 orders/month) ~65% of base
Large base, price-sensitive, non-habitual. Triggered by discounts alone.
Moderate Users (4–8 orders/month) TARGET ~25% of base
Convenience-driven, highest habit-forming potential, closest to Gold threshold.
Heavy Users (9+ orders/month) ~10% of base
Saturated, high AOV, engaged with Zomato Gold. Retain, don't over-invest.

Problem Statement

Order frequency among moderate users is not growing proportionally to Zomato's overall user base growth — leaving a large, addressable growth delta untapped.

Why It Matters

Each additional order from an existing user costs near-zero in acquisition cost, versus ₹300–500+ to acquire a new user. Frequency is a unit economics lever.

Root Cause Hypothesis

Zomato's product is optimized for first-order and re-activation via discounts — not for building habitual ordering behavior among mid-frequency users.

03
Research Product Teardown — Independent Analysis

What the product audit and public data reveal

Research Methodology: Public product audit of Zomato iOS/Android app; analysis of Zomato earnings reports and investor presentations (publicly available); competitive analysis of Swiggy, Uber Eats, and DoorDash; review of consumer behavior research on food delivery habits and habit formation psychology.
1

Zomato Gold users order 3–4x more frequently than non-subscribers

Publicly reported in Zomato investor presentations. This confirms that loyalty program engagement drives frequency — but Gold currently targets heavy users, not moderate users who could be converted.

Source: Zomato Investor Presentation (Public)
2

Zomato over-indexes on promotional discounts as an engagement trigger

Product audit finding: The primary re-engagement lever across push notifications, homepage banners, and browse surfaces is discount or coupon-driven. This trains users to wait for discounts rather than building habitual ordering behavior. The product is creating price-sensitive users, not loyal ones.

Source: Direct Product Audit (iOS/Android)
3

Uber Eats and DoorDash use scheduled ordering features that drive frequency

Both platforms offer planned/scheduled ordering flows that reduce in-session decision friction. DoorDash's "Scheduled Delivery" and Uber Eats' recurring order features target the medium-frequency cohort. Neither feature exists in Zomato's current product as of this audit.

Source: Competitive Product Audit
4

Food ordering habits follow routine triggers — time, location, day of week

Consumer behavior research on habit formation (Duhigg, Wood) identifies routine triggers as the primary driver of habitual behavior. Food ordering that becomes habitual is anchored to consistent time-of-day and contextual cues (office lunch, Friday dinner). Zomato's current product does not surface or reinforce these cues.

Source: Behavioral Research Review
04
Insight Product Teardown — Independent Analysis

The product is optimized for discovery, not habit

Three core insights emerge from the research — each pointing to the same root cause: Zomato's engagement model is transactional, not habitual. Solving for habit formation without increasing discount spend is the core PM opportunity.

01

Discount = Transaction, Not Habit

Every time Zomato triggers an order through a discount, it trains the user to wait for the next discount. The Pavlovian loop being built is: discount arrives → user orders. Remove the discount → no order. This is not habit formation. Habit formation requires non-discount triggers anchored to routine (time, place, social context).

02

The 'What Should I Order?' Problem is Unsolved

Moderate users spend significant time in the app deciding — browsing without ordering, or abandoning the app mid-session. Decision fatigue is a silent frequency killer. If Zomato eliminated the need to decide (via AI suggestions, saved plans, smart defaults), the natural order frequency would increase without any price incentive.

03

Social Proof & Shared Ordering Are Underutilized

Food is inherently social. Office lunch ordering, ordering with housemates, or sharing food plans with partners are all frequency-driving behaviors that Zomato barely supports. Group ordering, shared carts, and social proof features (friends ordered from here) are largely absent from the current product surface.

05
Opportunity Product Teardown — Independent Analysis

Build for routine, not for deals

The opportunity is clear: create habit-forming, non-discount features that build ordering routines for the moderate user segment, reducing the cost-per-engagement while increasing natural frequency.

Core Opportunity Statement

If Zomato invests in reducing in-session decision friction and introduces routine-building mechanics (meal planning, streak rewards, social ordering, smart reorder), the moderate user cohort will naturally increase order frequency — without incremental discount spend. This creates a better business (lower CAC, better unit economics) and a better product (genuinely useful, not just promotional).

Hypothesis

If Zomato reduces decision friction and introduces non-discount routine-building mechanics, moderate users will increase order frequency by 15–20% within 90 days of feature adoption — without additional discount spend.

Why This Scale of Opportunity

A 15% frequency increase across Zomato's moderate user base — approximately 4.25 million users — represents hundreds of crores in additional GMV at near-zero incremental CAC. Even a conservative 10% improvement at current average order values creates material business impact.

Why Now

Zomato's competitive moat is restaurant density and logistics. As Swiggy closes the gap on supply, product-led engagement differentiation becomes the next frontier. Habit mechanics cannot be copied overnight; they compound over time with user behavior data.

Prioritization Product Teardown — Independent Analysis

Four features, ordered by impact-to-effort

Using an impact vs. effort framework, four features are identified and sequenced. The goal is to maximize early signal (proving the hypothesis) while sequencing infrastructure-heavy features for later phases.

P0

Weekly AI Meal Planner

AI-generated weekly meal suggestions based on past orders, preferences, and time-of-day patterns. Directly addresses decision fatigue — the highest-friction moment in the moderate user journey. Users plan ahead; ordering becomes one tap at mealtime.

High Impact Medium Effort Addresses Core Insight #2
P1

Order Streak Rewards (Non-Discount)

Streak mechanics that reward consistent ordering with access and recognition — not discounts. 3-day streak: priority delivery. 7-day: early restaurant drops. 14-day: surprise dish. Builds habit through variable reward without eroding margins.

High Impact Low Effort Addresses Core Insight #1
P2

Social Ordering — Group Cart + Split Pay

Collaborative ordering: share a link, each person adds their item, one checkout, split payment. Targets the office lunch use case — a high-frequency, high-AOV, naturally social context. High infrastructure cost but high long-term value.

Medium Impact High Effort Addresses Core Insight #3
P3

Smart Reorder — One-Tap Contextual Suggestion

At 12:30 PM on a Tuesday, if the user has ordered from 'Subway near office' 6 times, show a one-tap banner: 'Your usual?' Leverages existing behavioral data to eliminate the decision entirely at the moment of highest ordering intent.

Medium Impact Low Effort Addresses Core Insight #2

Prioritization Rationale

Start with what reduces friction (Meal Planner) and builds habit without discounts (Streaks) — these prove the core hypothesis fastest. Smart Reorder is low effort but medium signal. Social Ordering requires payment infrastructure changes and is sequenced after proving the habit mechanics work. All four features compound: a user who plans meals is more likely to maintain a streak and reorder familiar meals.

07
Solution Product Teardown — Independent Analysis

Four product bets on habit formation

Each feature targets a specific friction point in the moderate user journey. Together, they create an ecosystem of habit: plan → order → reward → repeat.

01

Weekly AI Meal Planner

AI-generated weekly meal suggestions based on past order history, cuisine preferences, time-of-day patterns, and dietary inputs. Users 'plan' 3–5 meals for the week. At mealtime, a single tap converts the plan into an order. Decision fatigue is eliminated before it occurs.

01
02

Order Streak Rewards

Streak mechanics with non-discount rewards: 3-day streak → priority delivery. 7-day streak → early access to new restaurant drops. 14-day streak → surprise dish from a curated restaurant. No coupons. Access, recognition, and delight instead.

02
03

Social Ordering

'Ordering with colleagues?' — Share a link, each team member adds their item to a group cart, single checkout, split payment automatically. Targets the office lunch use case: a natural, repeated, high-AOV social ordering occasion that the product currently does not support.

03
04

Smart Reorder

At 12:30 PM on a Tuesday, if the user has ordered from 'Subway near office' 6 times before, the app surfaces a one-tap banner: 'Your usual order?' Tap to reorder. No browsing. No deciding. Zero friction at the exact moment of highest ordering intent — powered by existing behavioral data.

Metrics Product Teardown — Independent Analysis

What success looks like, measured precisely

A full metrics framework — from North Star to feature-level — ensures that each product bet is evaluated on its own terms while contributing to the top-line growth objective.

North Star Metric
Order Frequency — Moderate User Cohort (orders/user/month)

Baseline: 5.2 orders/month (estimated). Target: 6.0–6.2 orders/month within 90 days of feature adoption.

Meal Planner

  • Weekly plan completion rate
  • Plan-to-order conversion rate
  • Average plans created per user
  • Time-to-order from plan view (vs. baseline)

Order Streaks

  • Active streak users (%)
  • Streak retention: Day 7, Day 14, Day 30
  • Orders placed during streak vs. non-streak periods
  • Streak break recovery rate

Social Ordering

  • Social order GMV share (%)
  • Average group size per order
  • Repeat social order rate (D30)
  • Link share-to-join conversion rate

Smart Reorder

  • Reorder banner CTR
  • Reorder frequency per eligible user
  • Session-to-order time (vs. browse flow)
  • User satisfaction score post-reorder

Business Metrics

  • Moderate user D30 retention
  • Gold conversion rate (moderate cohort)
  • Average Order Value (AOV)
  • Discount-triggered order % (should decline)
  • GMV from moderate user cohort

Guardrail Metrics

  • Customer satisfaction / NPS (no regression)
  • App session abandonment rate
  • Support ticket volume (feature-related)
  • Restaurant partner experience scores

Experiment Product Teardown — Independent Analysis

Three phases, each earning the right to the next

Phased rollout with specific success thresholds per phase. Each phase tests the hypothesis independently before the next phase scales. No phase 2 without evidence from phase 1.

1

Meal Planner — Initial Rollout

Weeks 1–4 · 10% of moderate users · Mumbai

Roll out the AI Meal Planner to 10% of moderate users in Mumbai (highest food delivery density market). Measure plan completion rate and order frequency at D14 vs. matched control group. This tests whether reducing decision friction changes ordering behavior without any discount.

Key Measurement: Order frequency D14: Meal Planner cohort vs. control. Plan creation rate. Plan-to-order conversion.

Success Threshold

≥12% improvement in order frequency in the Meal Planner cohort at D14. If not met, diagnose: is the plan completion rate low (UX problem) or is plan-to-order failing (motivation problem)?

2

Order Streaks — A/B vs. Discount Equivalent

Weeks 5–8 · A/B Test · National

Run an A/B test pitting streak mechanics (non-discount rewards) against an equivalent discount spend directed at the same user cohort. This is the critical test: does a streak — offering access and recognition — drive equal or better frequency improvement than a discount of equivalent cost?

Key Measurement: D30 retention of streak users vs. discount users. Order frequency at week 8. Unit economics: CAC per incremental order.

Success Threshold

Streak users show ≥D30 retention equal to or better than discount users. If streaks drive equal frequency at lower cost, the business case for non-discount engagement is proven.

3

Social Ordering — Limited City Rollout

Weeks 9–16 · Top 5 Cities

Limited rollout of Social Ordering in top 5 cities by food delivery density (Bangalore, Mumbai, Delhi, Hyderabad, Pune). Target users with 3+ office-area orders in the last 30 days — proxy for the 'team lunch' use case. Measure group order adoption and repeat group order rate.

Key Measurement: Group order adoption (% of eligible users who place ≥1 group order). Repeat group order rate (D30). Average group size and GMV per group order vs. solo order.

Success Threshold

≥8% of eligible users place at least one group order. ≥40% of those repeat within 30 days. Group AOV ≥2x solo AOV (expected given group size).

Result Product Teardown — Independent Analysis

Projected outcomes and PM learnings

Product Teardown Disclaimer: All results below are projected based on research, public product audit, behavioral research, and competitive benchmarking. These are not measured outcomes. This is an independent analytical exercise demonstrating PM product sense and hypothesis-driven thinking.
15–20% Improvement in order frequency for the moderate user segment within 90 days of feature adoption
10% Improvement in Zomato Gold conversion from moderate user cohort, driven by habit proximity
↓ Reduction in discount-triggered order share — improved unit economics as habit replaces price sensitivity

PM Learnings from This Teardown

Learning 01

Frequency is not a discount problem — it's a habit problem

Discounts acquire behavior; habits retain it. Every time a product uses a discount to trigger usage, it creates a user who waits for the next discount. The highest-leverage engagement work is building routines that don't require financial incentives.

Learning 02

Decision fatigue kills consumer products silently

The best engagement feature is often not a new surface — it's removing the need to decide. Users who have to choose are users who might choose nothing. Reducing optionality at the right moment (Smart Reorder, Meal Planner) is more powerful than increasing it.

Learning 03

Always ask: who is the user growing from, and into?

Design for the trajectory, not the current state. The moderate user is not just a 5x/month orderer — they are a future Gold subscriber and heavy user. The product should design for who they're becoming, not just serve who they are. Segmenting by trajectory rather than snapshot changes every product decision.
