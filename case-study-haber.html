From Industrial Process
Pain Points to Product Impact

How customer discovery, product implementation, and structured feedback loops helped improve industrial process optimization at scale.

Role Product Analyst
Company Haber Technologies
Flagship Products Kaiznn · Mt. Fuji
Domain Industrial AI Automation / Water & Process Optimization
Period July 2022 – July 2025
01 · Executive Summary

What was the problem,
and what changed?

Industrial plants rely on accurate monitoring and precise dosing of process chemicals. In practice, operators and process teams face a compounding challenge: manual monitoring creates blind spots, inconsistent data makes root-cause analysis unreliable, and process variability means that even well-designed interventions fail to land without proper adoption infrastructure.

Haber Technologies builds products that automate monitoring and optimization of industrial processes — replacing manual, reactive workflows with real-time intelligence and automated dosing decisions. As a Product Analyst at Haber (July 2022 – July 2025), I owned the end-to-end product lifecycle for two flagship products — Kaiznn and Mt. Fuji — working across customer discovery, requirements definition (PRDs), roadmap prioritization, and ML model validation.

My Contribution

I led Voice-of-Customer research through 100+ on-site customer discovery interactions; synthesized pain points into structured PRDs that shaped the roadmap for two flagship features; partnered with data science on ML model validation for chemical dosing automation (improving accuracy by 8% and reducing chemical costs by 12–15%); shipped operational analytics dashboards tracking 15+ KPIs; and authored customer onboarding SOPs that reduced new site onboarding time by 30% across 5+ newly commissioned sites.

02 · The Problem

Industrial optimization is not just a data problem.
It's an adoption problem.

Industrial process optimization sits at the intersection of operations, data, and human behavior. Each of these introduces its own category of friction.

Where friction occurs in the standard workflow

Plant Operations
→
Sensors / Manual Data
→
Process Monitoring
→
Decision-Making
→
Chemical Dosing
→
Operational Outcome
Friction points Automation-enabled stage
Operational

Multiple Process Parameters

Plants track pH, turbidity, flow rate, conductivity, and more simultaneously. Manual tracking across these dimensions is cognitively demanding and error-prone.

Data

Inconsistent Operational Data

Sensor drift, maintenance gaps, and manual entry errors compound to produce unreliable data that makes trend analysis and root-cause investigation difficult.

Human

Operator Dependency

Process decisions rely heavily on individual operator experience. Knowledge is not systematically captured, making outcomes inconsistent across shifts and sites.

Adoption

Long Onboarding Cycles

Each plant has a unique configuration. Without a structured onboarding framework, time-to-value was extended and implementation quality varied significantly site by site.

Translation

Data Without Decisions

Process data was often available, but difficult to convert into specific, actionable recommendations. Operators knew something was wrong — not always what to do about it.

Feedback

Weak Customer–Product Loop

Operational feedback from plants rarely made it to product teams in a structured form. Problems were addressed reactively, rather than systematically incorporated into the product roadmap.

03 · Users & Stakeholders

Who uses the product —
and who it ultimately serves

Industrial B2B products have layered stakeholders. The person who operates the product is rarely the person who procures it. Understanding this separation is fundamental to effective product work in this space.

Primary Users

Primary User

Plant Operator

Goal

Reliable, easy day-to-day process operation without ambiguity or overload.

Pain Point

Excessive manual monitoring across multiple parameters; difficulty acting on incomplete or inconsistent data.

Success Looks Like

Clear, actionable recommendations. Fewer process surprises. Confidence in the system's output.

Primary User

Process Engineer

Goal

Process stability, deviation detection, and systematic optimization.

Pain Point

Difficulty identifying deviations early. Limited visibility into historical trends. Knowledge not captured systematically.

Success Looks Like

Better diagnostic visibility, faster deviation response, and continuous process improvement over time.

Primary User

Site Head

Goal

Cost reduction, operational efficiency, and reliable KPI reporting to leadership.

Pain Point

Lack of consolidated, plant-level visibility. Dependent on operator and engineer reports that may lag or be inconsistent.

Success Looks Like

Measurable, reportable plant-level improvement. Reduced chemical costs. Reduced operational variability.

Secondary Stakeholders

Secondary

Plant Owner / Management

Ultimately accountable for capital and operational costs. Needs confidence that technology investment produces a measurable return. Rarely interacts with the product directly.

Secondary

Purchase / Procurement

Evaluates vendor credentials, contract terms, and total cost of ownership. Influences initial purchase decisions but is not involved in daily product use.

Secondary

Customer Success / Implementation

Responsible for deployment quality and time-to-value. Carries the operational risk of failed implementations. A key source of structured customer feedback.

Secondary

Product & Engineering Teams

Build and iterate on the product. Dependent on accurate, structured customer input to prioritize improvements correctly. Often separated from the plant floor by multiple layers.

Customers don't buy dashboards — they buy operational outcomes. The product had to answer: "What should I do right now?"

Product adoption in industrial settings depends heavily on onboarding quality and early trust-building, not just feature completeness.

05 · Customer Pain Points

Observed friction points,
structured as product problems

The following pain points emerged consistently across implementation engagements. They are documented as operational observations, not inferred hypotheses.

01
Manual Process Monitoring
Current Workflow

Operators manually log process parameters across shifts using physical logs or basic spreadsheets, often with long intervals between checks.

Impact

Slow detection of deviations. High operator cognitive load. Inconsistent data quality across shifts and sites.

Product Opportunity → Automated real-time monitoring with exception-based alerting, reducing manual observation burden.
02
Data Quality Issues
Current Workflow

Sensor drift, calibration gaps, and manual data entry errors produce data that is inconsistent or unreliable, especially over longer time windows.

Impact

Repeated implementation feedback indicated that poor data quality was a root cause of incorrect dosing decisions and eroded operator trust in the system.

Product Opportunity → In-system data validation, anomaly flagging, and calibration alerts to improve data reliability at the source.
03
Long Onboarding & Setup
Current Workflow

Each plant deployment was handled largely on a site-by-site basis, with significant variation in setup time, configuration approach, and documentation quality.

Impact

Extended time-to-value. Inconsistent implementation quality. Pressure on implementation resources across concurrent deployments.

Product Opportunity → Standardized onboarding templates and configuration workflows to accelerate time-to-live.
04
Interpreting Process Data
Current Workflow

Process data was available but presented in ways that required engineering expertise to interpret, limiting its usefulness for frontline operators.

Impact

Data without decision support creates friction. Operators who couldn't interpret data defaulted to manual interventions, reducing automation benefit.

Product Opportunity → Contextual recommendations layered over raw data — "what to do" rather than "what is happening."
05
Process Variability Across Sites
Current Workflow

Each plant has unique configurations, inlet water quality, and process parameters. Solutions designed for one site did not transfer cleanly to another.

Impact

High variability in implementation effort and post-deployment performance. Product configurations required extensive customization per site.

Product Opportunity → Configurable templates and site-specific parameter tuning to balance scalability with local adaptation.
06
Customer–Product Feedback Gap
Current Workflow

Operational feedback from the field was often shared informally and did not consistently reach product teams in a structured or prioritized form.

Impact

Recurring issues remained unresolved across multiple deployments. High-impact improvements were missed because the signal wasn't reaching the roadmap.

Product Opportunity → A structured implementation feedback loop with categorized issue tracking and escalation to product teams.

10 · PM Learnings

What this experience taught me
about Product Management

1

Start with the workflow, not the feature.

In industrial settings, a feature that doesn't fit the operator's shift rhythm doesn't get used — regardless of its technical quality. Understanding the existing workflow before proposing a solution is not optional. It is the foundation of effective product thinking in this domain.

2

Customer discovery continues after launch.

The most specific and actionable product insights emerge from real usage, not from pre-deployment interviews. Structured post-deployment observation reveals use cases, failure modes, and unmet needs that no research framework anticipates in advance.

3

Implementation is part of the product experience.

How a product is deployed shapes how it is perceived and adopted. A difficult onboarding process creates doubt about the product itself. Treating implementation quality as a product requirement — not just an operational concern — materially affects adoption outcomes.

4

Data is useful only when it drives action.

Industrial operators are surrounded by data. The product's job is not to show more data — it is to surface the decision that needs to be made right now. Displaying information and enabling action are two different product outcomes. Building for the latter is significantly harder.

5

The best product decisions happen at the intersection of customer pain, technical feasibility, and business impact.

In B2B industrial tech, it's easy to optimize for what's technically elegant, or what the customer loudly requests, or what has the most commercial upside — in isolation. The highest-value decisions consistently emerge when all three signals align. Learning to read that intersection is a core PM skill.

11 · If I Were the PM

Proposed improvements based
on observed operational gaps

The following are my proposed product improvements — derived from direct implementation experience. These are not existing product capabilities. They represent where I believe the product could evolve based on observed customer pain.

Guided Onboarding Framework

Problem

Each site deployment required significant manual effort with high variability in quality and time.

Hypothesis

A structured, in-product onboarding checklist with site-type templates would reduce setup variability and time.

Proposed Solution

Template-based onboarding flows with pre-configured parameter defaults by plant type, validation checkpoints, and documentation generation.

Expected Impact

Further reduction in onboarding time; more consistent deployment quality across sites.

Success Metric → Time-to-live per site; implementation CSAT

Automated Anomaly Detection & Alerting

Problem

Process deviations were often identified only after the fact, when operators noticed the outcome rather than the cause.

Hypothesis

Real-time anomaly detection with contextual alerts (not just threshold breaches) would enable earlier intervention.

Proposed Solution

ML-based deviation detection surfacing operator-readable alerts with recommended action — not just raw sensor values.

Expected Impact

Faster deviation response; reduced downstream chemical waste from undetected process drift.

Success Metric → Mean time to detect deviation; alert action rate

Customer Health & Adoption Dashboard

Problem

Internal teams had limited visibility into whether customers were actively using the product and deriving value from it.

Hypothesis

A usage and adoption dashboard for customer success and product teams would enable proactive intervention before churn or disengagement.

Proposed Solution

Internal product health dashboard tracking active usage, recommendation acceptance rate, alert resolution, and data quality by site.

Expected Impact

Earlier detection of at-risk customers; ability to prioritize implementation support where it's most needed.

Success Metric → Feature adoption rate; recommendation acceptance rate per site

Process Performance Benchmarking

Problem

Site heads had no external reference point for whether their process performance was good, average, or below par.

Hypothesis

Anonymized cross-site benchmarking would increase engagement from site heads and create internal motivation for optimization.

Proposed Solution

Opt-in benchmarking module showing how a site's key metrics compare to similar plant configurations (anonymized, aggregated).

Expected Impact

Increased site head engagement; stronger business case for product expansion across plants.

Success Metric → Site head login frequency; expansion conversations initiated

Structured Implementation Feedback Loop

Problem

Field feedback was captured informally and inconsistently, limiting its ability to systematically improve the product.

Hypothesis

A formalized, categorized feedback intake process tied to deployment milestones would surface high-impact issues faster.

Proposed Solution

Structured post-deployment review checklist with categorized issue tagging, severity scoring, and direct routing to product backlog.

Expected Impact

Higher quality product input from field; reduction in repeat issues across deployments.

Success Metric → % of field issues reaching product backlog; repeat-issue rate
